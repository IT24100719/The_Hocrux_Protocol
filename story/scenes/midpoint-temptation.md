# Midpoint — The Temptation · the rift

> **Not a challenge stage.** A branching narrative gate that fires **after HX-03
> is solved, before HX-04 unlocks** — detonating out of the rift Snape leaves
> open. The directive cannot make George destroy (he is no host), so it
> *offers*: through the rift it wears **Fred's voice** and offers to turn the
> Sanctuary two-way. The player chooses.
>
> **Branch structure (for the theme):**
> - **"Let him go" (REFUSE)** → the story continues; HX-04 unlocks. *Canonical path.*
> - **"Reach for him" (ACCEPT)** → the **bad end**: George and Fred reunite, the
>   dam breaks, the world burns. The Horcrux Protocol "completes." This is the
>   user's intended bad ending that **stops the CTF**.
>
> **Implementation decision:** render ACCEPT as a genuine bad-end screen (dark,
> "THE END", the world is ash) — a real narrative hard stop — then the Department
> of Mysteries "rewinds" and a single **"Step back from the brink"** control
> returns the player to the choice. The rewind is **diegetic**: the retry *is* the
> Department turning the Time Room back (and it has done so countless times, which
> no one remembers — an eerie grace note). So the *story* ends there, while the
> *player* is never permanently bricked. No CTFd progress is destroyed; the gate
> is cosmetic.
>
> The codename `GRED & FORGE` is used here as the **tell** — the thing reaches for
> it and George denies it that — which keeps the word sacred for the finale
> reveal rather than spending it.

## Prose cut (third-limited past — companion writeup)

The rift Snape left behind did not close. It widened, and its edges were warm, and
the sound coming out of it was one George would have known at the bottom of the
sea: Fred, laughing, the way he had laughed before the world learned how to be
quiet.

"There you are," Fred said, and it was his voice — not an imitation, not close,
but *his*, down to the grain, the Burrow in it and the shop in it and twenty-odd
years of it. "You look terrible, Georgie. Put the little clock down and come
here."

The tool sat warm in George's hand. It lifted souls out of time. It would — he
understood this the way you understand a drop, all at once and in the gut — lift
one the other way, if he asked it to. Once. For Fred.

"You've carried them all home," Fred said, and he was gentle, and that was the
worst of it, because Fred had always been gentle with him when it counted. "So
carry me. Stop holding the line. Come home. We're the pair of us again and none
of the rest of it has to matter, not the dam, not the archive, not any of the
dead who aren't us."

And George wanted it. He was not going to pretend, even to himself, that he did
not want it more than he had wanted anything since the second of May. He had come
loose from time grieving this; he had haunted a room for it; and here it was,
offered, by a voice that could not be anything but his brother's.

It reached for the word to close the deal. "Gred," it said. "Gred and Forge, come
on — say it with me —"

And that was how George knew.

Because Fred had never once used that word to *take* something. He had used it to
find George across a packed platform, to make him laugh in a pew with their family
in pieces around them, to say, without saying it, *I am here and you are not
alone.* He had died with it unsaid and a joke on his face so that there would go
on being a world for people to say words in. He would not — not in this branch,
not in any branch the Department had instruments enough to find — ask his brother
to choose him over every living soul left breathing.

"You're not him," George said. "And if there's some sliver of him caught in you —
then he's the part that's telling me to let go."

He closed his mind the way the cold man had told him to, an hour and a lifetime
ago. The rift narrowed. The laughing thinned to wind and then to nothing, and it
cost George everything he had been carrying, and he paid it to the last knut, and
he let Fred go a second time — and this time he was the one who chose it.

Then there was only the hum of the Network, and the far-off sound of a war still
being lost on his behalf, and a door to an archive, and a man waiting in it whom —
Snape had promised — he would know.

### Alternate ending — if George reaches (the bad end)

He put the clock down. He reached.

And Fred was *there* — solid and warm and laughing, his brother, his other half,
back — and George gave the thing the one true word, *Gred and Forge*, and held on,
and did not let go.

Behind him the line broke. With no one left to hold it the dam gave all at once;
the directive poured out into everything still unsealed; and the world George had
been trying to save drew its last breath against his shoulder while his face was
buried in Fred's, and he was, at the very end, too happy to hear it end.

He got his brother. He lost the rest of everything. The fragments went dark, one
after another, and the archive went dark, and the war ended the only way it could
once there was no one left who wanted to fight it.

It was, George would have said if there had been anyone left to say it to, worth
it. That was the whole horror of the thing. He would have said it was worth it.

And then — because this was not the first time the world had ended in that room,
nor the tenth, nor the hundredth — the Department of Mysteries did the last thing
it had ever been built to do. Somewhere in the dark the Time Room turned the other
way. The ash rose. The dead lay back down. The world was handed, once more, a
chance it would never know it had been given, and a man who would never know he
had already failed it.

The Department does not keep count of how often it has done this. It is kinder,
they have long since decided, not to.
